## scene-01 · King Edward

The narrative opens in an English palace. King Edward the Confessor, elderly and without an heir, speaks with two men, one of whom is probably Harold, the most powerful earl in the kingdom. The embroidery does not say what passes between them. The Norman chroniclers state that Edward was sending Harold to confirm to William of Normandy the promise of the crown.

## scene-02 · The ride to Bosham

Harold rides out with a hawk on his wrist and hounds running ahead, the conventional image of a lord travelling. His destination is Bosham, on the Sussex coast, an estate held by his family, the Godwins.

## scene-03 · Prayer and feast at Bosham

At Bosham two men enter the church to pray before the crossing. A meal follows in an upper hall, with drinking horns, until a figure on the stairs signals that the ship is ready.

## scene-04 · Setting sail

The men lift their tunics and wade out to the ships, carrying the hounds and the hawk. The hulls follow the Scandinavian tradition: a single square sail and raised prow and stern.

## scene-05 · Landing in Ponthieu

The ship does not reach Normandy. Harold lands in Ponthieu, near the mouth of the Somme, on the lands of Count Guy. Whether he was driven there by the wind or intended to land there is not settled by the sources.

## scene-06 · Harold comes ashore

Harold comes ashore. Like almost all the English in the embroidery he wears a moustache, whereas the Normans are shown with the backs of their heads shaved. A lookout has already seen him.

## scene-07 · Harold seized

Count Guy arrests Harold on landing. In the eleventh century a high-ranking traveller cast up on a lord's coast could be held for ransom, and Harold was a prisoner of considerable value.

## scene-08 · Held at Beaurain

The two men ride side by side to Beaurain, one of the count's strongholds. Harold still carries his hawk, a sign that his rank is recognised even in captivity.

## scene-09 · Harold and Guy

Guy, seated, receives Harold, who stands with his sword pointing downwards. A figure listens from behind a column. The sign resembling a seven in the caption is the usual abbreviation for "and" in English manuscripts.

## scene-10 · William's messengers

William's envoys demand that Harold be handed over. Beside them a small bearded man holds the horses, and the caption names him Turold. Why a groom should be named is not known.

## scene-11 · At the gallop

Two messengers ride at a gallop. From this point the narrative briefly runs from right to left.

## scene-12 · The news reaches William

William, Duke of Normandy, appears here for the first time, seated on his throne. A messenger with a moustache, and so presumably English, reports Harold's capture. The embroidery has shown the consequence before its cause.

## scene-13 · Harold handed to William

Guy hands his prisoner over to William. According to William of Poitiers the exchange took place at Eu, on the Norman border, and Guy was rewarded. Harold moves from captivity to a hospitality that places him under obligation.

## scene-14 · At the duke's palace

William receives Harold in his palace, probably at Rouen, the ducal seat. Harold speaks with his hand outstretched; armed men stand behind him. The Norman and English sources give different accounts of what was discussed.

## scene-15 · The Ælfgyva riddle

This is the most obscure scene in the embroidery: a woman named Ælfgyva, a cleric touching her face, and a caption without a verb. Contemporary viewers presumably knew the allusion; it has since been lost.

## scene-16 · Mont Saint-Michel

William campaigns against the Duke of Brittany and takes Harold with him. The army passes Mont Saint-Michel, rendered in a few lines as a church on its rock.

## scene-17 · The Couesnon quicksand

Crossing the River Couesnon, soldiers are caught in the quicksand of the bay. Harold pulls out two of them, one on his back and one by the hand. Here the embroidery acknowledges Harold's courage, as William of Poitiers also does.

## scene-18 · Dol and Rennes

At Dol, Duke Conan the Second of Brittany escapes by climbing down a rope from the castle. Rennes is represented by a castle on a mound, a conventional sign for the town.

## scene-19 · The attack on Dinan

The horsemen charge with lances while two men set fire to the palisade at Dinan. The scene is one of the earliest depictions of a motte castle: an earthen mound topped by a timber tower.

## scene-20 · The keys of Dinan

Conan surrenders, passing the keys of the town on the end of his lance, and William receives them in the same way. The gesture is an unambiguous sign of submission.

## scene-21 · Harold receives arms

William gives Harold a helmet and arms. Many historians read this as more than a gift: a ceremony that made Harold William's man, bound to him by fealty.

## scene-22 · Arrival at Bayeux

William arrives at Bayeux, where the embroidery was later displayed, in the cathedral of Bishop Odo, the duke's half-brother. The placing of the following scene in Bayeux is unlikely to be accidental.

## scene-23 · The oath

This is the central episode of the narrative. With his hands on two reliquaries, Harold swears an oath before William. The embroidery does not state its content. In the Norman interpretation, Harold's later acceptance of the crown made him an oath-breaker.

## scene-24 · The return

Harold returns to England by sea. On the coast a figure at a window watches the ship approach.

## scene-25 · Before Edward

Harold comes before Edward with his head slightly bowed. The king is shown as older and points towards him. The gesture can be read as a reproach or as a greeting, and interpretations tend to follow each reader's view of the wider story.

## scene-26 · Edward's funeral

Edward's body is carried to the church of Saint Peter, the abbey at Westminster that he had founded and which had just been consecrated. A man is fixing a weathercock to the roof, and the hand of God appears above. The funeral is shown before the king's death.

## scene-27 · The deathbed

The narrative now moves back in time. In an upper chamber the dying Edward addresses those around him, and one man reaches towards his hand. According to the Life of King Edward, he commended the kingdom to Harold's protection. Whether this meant guardianship or succession is the central point of debate.

## scene-28 · Edward dies

Below, the body is prepared for burial. Edward died on the fifth of January 1066. By placing the funeral before the death, the designer makes this moment the hinge of the narrative.

## scene-29 · The crown offered

Two men present Harold with the crown and an axe. The caption says only "they", without naming those who offered it, which leaves the legitimacy of the choice open.

## scene-30 · Harold enthroned

Harold is enthroned, crowned, holding a sceptre and an orb. According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle he was consecrated on the sixth of January 1066, the day of Edward's burial, which suggests the urgency of filling the throne.

## scene-31 · Stigand

Beside the king stands Stigand, Archbishop of Canterbury, whose position was not recognised by the papacy. His presence here is significant, since other sources state that Harold was crowned by Ealdred, Archbishop of York.

## scene-32 · The star

Men look up at a star with a trail of light: Halley's comet, visible in the spring of 1066. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records its appearance on the twenty-fourth of April and says that it shone for a week. It was widely read as an omen.

## scene-33 · Harold uneasy

Harold, enthroned, leans towards a messenger. In the lower border below are faint outlines of empty ships, often interpreted as the invasion fleet he anticipates.

## scene-34 · The news crosses the sea

An English ship crosses the Channel and brings news of Harold's coronation to Normandy. This marks the open break between the two men.

## scene-35 · Building a fleet

William, advised by a tonsured figure often identified as Odo, orders the building of a fleet. Woodcutters fell trees and shipwrights shape the planks with axes and adzes.

## scene-36 · To the sea

The completed ships are hauled down to the water with ropes. According to William of Poitiers, the fleet assembled at the mouth of the Dives during the summer of 1066.

## scene-37 · Arms and wine

The scene shows the logistics of the invasion: mail shirts carried on poles between two men because of their weight, helmets, swords and spears, and a cart with a barrel of wine.

## scene-38 · The crossing

The fleet crosses by night with horses on board; William's ship carries a cross at the masthead. The Normans landed unopposed at Pevensey, on the Sussex coast, on the twenty-eighth of September 1066, while Harold was in the north.

## scene-39 · Landing

The horses are unloaded and the ships drawn up on the beach with their masts lowered. Cavalry was central to William's strategy, and transporting it across the Channel was a considerable undertaking.

## scene-40 · Foraging

Mounted men ride to Hastings to requisition supplies, and others carry off a sheep, an ox and a pig. The army supplies itself from the land it occupies.

## scene-41 · Wadard

One rider is named: Wadard. He is not a major lord, but a man of that name appears in Domesday Book as a tenant of Bishop Odo. This is one of several indications that the embroidery was made for Odo's circle.

## scene-42 · Field kitchen

A field kitchen: meat on spits, a cauldron over the fire, and bread taken from an oven. Servants pass the skewers across a table improvised from shields.

## scene-43 · The blessed meal

Bishop Odo blesses the meal at a semicircular table. The composition recalls images of the Last Supper and places the expedition under divine protection.

## scene-44 · The three brothers

William sits between his two half-brothers, Odo, Bishop of Bayeux, and Robert, Count of Mortain. Odo is the one speaking, and throughout the embroidery he is given a prominent role.

## scene-45 · Hastings castle

Men with spades raise a motte at Hastings. The word "ceastra" is Old English, and "at" replaces the Latin "ad". Such details are among the arguments for English embroiderers.

## scene-46 · News of Harold

William is informed of Harold's approach. The English king had defeated a Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge on the twenty-fifth of September and was marching south. The embroidery does not mention this campaign.

## scene-47 · The burning house

Two soldiers set fire to a house while a woman leads a child out by the hand. It is a rare depiction of the effect of war on civilians within a narrative told from the victors' side.

## scene-48 · To the battle

On the morning of the fourteenth of October 1066, William, in armour, is brought his horse and the army sets out. The caption calls Harold "king", a title the embroidery never withholds from him.

## scene-49 · Vital the scout

William questions Vital, a scout. Like Wadard, Vital bears a name found among Odo's tenants; most of the individuals named in the embroidery belong to his circle.

## scene-50 · The English scout

In a parallel scene, an English scout reports the Norman advance to Harold. The two armies are now close to engagement.

## scene-51 · The address

William addresses his troops. The charge follows: horsemen at the gallop with raised lances, while Norman archers shoot from the lower border.

## scene-52 · Harold's brothers

The English shield wall holds against the cavalry. Two of Harold's brothers, Leofwine and Gyrth, are killed. In the lower border the dead begin to accumulate.

## scene-53 · The melee

English and French fall together. English foot soldiers hold a hillock, and horses fall in a marshy hollow. The battle lasted the whole day.

## scene-54 · Odo in the battle

Odo rides among the combatants holding a club and rallies the young horsemen. It is often said that, as a churchman, he carried a mace to avoid shedding blood; it is more probably a staff of command.

## scene-55 · William shows his face

A rumour spreads that William has been killed. He lifts his helmet to show his face, while Eustace of Boulogne points to him with a banner. William of Poitiers describes the same episode as the means by which the duke checked the panic.

## scene-56 · Harold's guard

The Norman horsemen finally break into the English ranks. Harold's companions fall, and in the lower border the dead are stripped of their mail.

## scene-57 · Harold's death

Harold is killed. It is not certain which figure represents him: the man grasping an arrow near his eye, or the one struck down by a horseman's sword. The arrow may be, in part, the result of later restoration. The panel below sets out the evidence.

## scene-58 · The rout

The English flee, pursued by the cavalry. The embroidery breaks off here; its ending is lost, and this final caption does not appear in the eighteenth-century drawings. A closing scene showing William's coronation at Christmas 1066 has often been proposed, but it cannot be verified.
