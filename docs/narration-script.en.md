## scene-01 · King Edward

It all begins in an English palace. The old, childless king, Edward the Confessor, talks with two men; one is probably Harold, the most powerful earl in the kingdom. What is he telling him? The embroidery keeps silent. The Norman chroniclers do not: Edward, they say, is sending Harold to confirm the promise of the crown to William of Normandy.

## scene-02 · The ride to Bosham

Harold rides out with a hawk on his wrist and his hounds running ahead: the picture of a great lord travelling, not of a man on a secret errand. He is heading for Bosham, on the Sussex coast, an estate of his family, the Godwins.

## scene-03 · Prayer and feast at Bosham

At Bosham two men enter the church to pray before the crossing. Then comes a feast in an upstairs hall, drinking horns raised, until a man on the stairs calls them down: the ship is ready.

## scene-04 · Setting sail

The men hitch up their tunics and wade out, carrying the hounds and the hawk, to reach the ships. Look at the hulls: these are boats in the Scandinavian tradition, with a square sail and a raised prow and stern.

## scene-05 · Landing in Ponthieu

The crossing does not end in Normandy. Harold lands in Ponthieu, near the mouth of the Somme, on the lands of Count Guy. Was he blown off course, or did he mean to land here? The sources do not agree.

## scene-06 · Harold comes ashore

Harold steps ashore. Like almost every Englishman in the embroidery he wears a moustache, while the Normans have the backs of their heads shaved. A lookout has already spotted him.

## scene-07 · Harold seized

Count Guy arrests Harold as soon as he lands. In the eleventh century a high-ranking traveller cast up on your shore could be worth a ransom: the count of Ponthieu has made a valuable catch.

## scene-08 · Held at Beaurain

The two riders head side by side for Beaurain, one of the count's strongholds. Harold still carries his hawk: a captive, but still a nobleman.

## scene-09 · Harold and Guy

Guy, seated, receives Harold, who stands with his sword pointing down. Behind a column, a man is listening. The sign "⁊" in the caption is shorthand for "and", common in English manuscripts.

## scene-10 · William's messengers

William's envoys demand that Harold be handed over. Beside them a small bearded man holds their horses, and the embroidery gives him a name: Turold. Why name a groom? Nobody knows for sure.

## scene-11 · At the gallop

Two riders at full gallop, hair streaming. Careful: from here, for a moment, the story reads from right to left.

## scene-12 · The news reaches William

Here, for the first time, is William, Duke of Normandy, on his throne. A moustached messenger, so an Englishman, brings news of Harold's capture. This is what set off the previous scenes: the embroidery has shown the effect before the cause.

## scene-13 · Harold handed to William

Guy hands over his prisoner. According to William of Poitiers the exchange took place at Eu, on the Norman border, and Guy was rewarded. Harold passes from captivity into a hospitality that puts him in William's debt.

## scene-14 · At the duke's palace

William receives Harold in his palace, probably at Rouen, the ducal capital. Harold speaks, hand outstretched, with armed men behind him. What are they bargaining over? The Norman and the English sources tell two different stories.

## scene-15 · The Ælfgyva riddle

Here is the most mysterious scene in the embroidery. A woman named Ælfgyva, a cleric touching her face, and a sentence with no verb. Eleventh-century viewers probably knew the story; we have lost it.

## scene-16 · Mont Saint-Michel

William goes to war against the Duke of Brittany and takes Harold with him. The army passes below Mont Saint-Michel, drawn in a few lines: the church on top of its rock.

## scene-17 · The Couesnon quicksand

Crossing the Couesnon, soldiers sink into the quicksand of the bay. Harold rescues two of them, one on his back and one by the hand. Here the Norman story honours the Englishman's courage, as William of Poitiers does too.

## scene-18 · Dol and Rennes

At Dol, Duke Conan II of Brittany escapes by sliding down a rope from his castle. Further on, Rennes is shown as a castle on its mound: a whole city reduced to a sign.

## scene-19 · The attack on Dinan

The horsemen charge with lances levelled while two men set fire to the palisade of Dinan's castle. It is one of the earliest pictures of a motte castle: an earth mound crowned with a wooden tower.

## scene-20 · The keys of Dinan

Conan surrenders, passing the keys of the town on the tip of his lance, and William takes them the same way. A gesture of surrender anyone could understand.

## scene-21 · Harold receives arms

William gives Harold a helmet and arms. For many historians this is more than a gift: it is a gesture that makes Harold William's man, bound to him by loyalty.

## scene-22 · Arrival at Bayeux

William comes to Bayeux, the city where the embroidery would later hang, in the cathedral of Bishop Odo, the duke's half-brother. It is probably no accident that the next, decisive scene takes place here.

## scene-23 · The oath

The heart of the story. With his hands on two reliquaries, Harold swears an oath before William. What he swore, the embroidery does not say. But to the Normans, by taking the crown, Harold will become an oath-breaker.

## scene-24 · The return

Harold sails home to England. On the shore a lookout at a window watches the ship come in, as if his return were awaited anxiously.

## scene-25 · Before Edward

Harold comes before Edward, head slightly bowed. The king looks older and points at him. A reproach? A simple greeting? Everyone reads this posture according to the story they believe.

## scene-26 · Edward's funeral

Edward's body is carried to the church of St Peter, the Westminster Abbey he had built, newly consecrated. A man is still fixing a weathercock on the roof, and the hand of God reaches down from the sky. But wait: the king has not died yet.

## scene-27 · The deathbed

The embroidery steps back in time. In the upper room the dying Edward speaks to those around him, and one man reaches out to touch his hand. According to the Life of King Edward, he commends the kingdom to Harold's protection. Entrusted, or bequeathed? That is the whole debate.

## scene-28 · Edward dies

Just below, the body is being prepared for burial. Edward died on 5 January 1066. The layout, funeral first and death after, turns his death into the hinge of the whole story.

## scene-29 · The crown offered

Two men offer Harold the crown and an axe. Note the "they" in the caption: who are "they"? The embroidery leaves the legitimacy of the choice in doubt.

## scene-30 · Harold enthroned

Harold sits enthroned, crowned, holding sceptre and orb. According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle he was consecrated on 6 January 1066, the very day of Edward's burial. The haste shows the urgency: the kingdom could not be left without a king.

## scene-31 · Stigand

Beside the king stands Stigand, Archbishop of Canterbury, whose position the papacy disputed. Showing him here is not neutral: other sources say Harold was crowned by Ealdred, Archbishop of York.

## scene-32 · The star

Men look up at a "star" with flowing hair: Halley's comet, which crossed the sky in the spring of 1066. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle has it appear on 24 April and shine for a week. To everyone it was an omen.

## scene-33 · Harold uneasy

Harold, on his throne, leans towards a messenger. Just below, in the border, float empty ships, barely outlined. Many see in them the fleet Harold already fears.

## scene-34 · The news crosses the sea

An English ship crosses the Channel bringing news of Harold's coronation to Normandy. The break is complete.

## scene-35 · Building a fleet

William, advised by a tonsured man often identified as Odo, orders a fleet to be built. Woodcutters fell trees; shipwrights shape planks with axe and adze.

## scene-36 · To the sea

The finished ships are hauled down to the water on ropes. According to William of Poitiers the fleet gathered at the mouth of the Dives during the summer of 1066.

## scene-37 · Arms and wine

The logistics of an invasion: mail shirts carried on a pole between two men, so heavy are they, helmets, swords, lances, and a cart loaded with a barrel of wine.

## scene-38 · The crossing

The fleet crosses by night, horses on board. William's ship has a cross at the masthead. The Normans land at Pevensey, on the Sussex coast, on 28 September 1066, unopposed: Harold is away in the north.

## scene-39 · Landing

The horses are unloaded, then the ships are beached with their masts lowered. The Norman cavalry is the decisive weapon of the expedition, and it had to be carried across the Channel alive.

## scene-40 · Foraging

The knights race to Hastings to requisition food. Men carry off a sheep, an ox, a pig: an army lives off the land it occupies.

## scene-41 · Wadard

One rider is named: Wadard. He is no great lord, yet a man of that name appears in Domesday Book as a tenant of Bishop Odo. One more hint that the embroidery was made for Odo's circle.

## scene-42 · Field kitchen

An open-air kitchen: meat on spits, a cauldron hanging over the fire, loaves taken from an oven. Servants pass the skewers across a makeshift table made of shields.

## scene-43 · The blessed meal

Around a half-moon table, Bishop Odo blesses the meal. The layout recalls pictures of the Last Supper: the conquest is placed under God's protection.

## scene-44 · The three brothers

William sits between his two half-brothers: Odo, Bishop of Bayeux, and Robert, Count of Mortain. Odo is the one speaking. In this embroidery he is never a bystander.

## scene-45 · Hastings castle

Men with spades throw up a motte at Hastings. The word "ceastra" is Old English, and "at" stands in for Latin "ad": small clues that point to English embroiderers.

## scene-46 · News of Harold

William is told that Harold is coming. The English king has just defeated a Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge, on 25 September, and is marching south at speed. The embroidery says not a word about it.

## scene-47 · The burning house

Two soldiers set fire to a house; a woman leads a child out by the hand. A rare picture of war seen from the civilians' side, in the middle of a victor's story.

## scene-48 · To the battle

On the morning of 14 October 1066 William, in armour, is brought his horse and the army sets out. Note the caption: Harold is called "king". The embroidery never takes that title away from him.

## scene-49 · Vital the scout

William questions Vital, a scout. Like Wadard, Vital bears a name found among Odo's tenants. The men named in the embroidery nearly all come from his circle.

## scene-50 · The English scout

As a mirror image, an English scout reports the Normans' approach to Harold. The two armies are closing on each other; battle is imminent.

## scene-51 · The address

William addresses his troops. Then the charge: riders at the gallop, lances raised, while the Norman archers loose their arrows in the lower border.

## scene-52 · Harold's brothers

The English shield wall holds against the horsemen. But two of Harold's brothers, Leofwine and Gyrth, are killed. In the border the dead are already piling up.

## scene-53 · The melee

English and "French" die side by side. On a hillock English foot soldiers hold out; horses crash and tumble in a marshy hollow. The battle lasts all day.

## scene-54 · Odo in the battle

Odo rides into the fray, club in hand, rallying the young horsemen who are wavering. It is often said that he carries a mace so as not to shed blood as a churchman; it is more likely a staff of command.

## scene-55 · William shows his face

A rumour spreads that William is dead. He pushes up his helmet to show his face, while Eustace of Boulogne points to him with his banner. William of Poitiers tells the same story: this is how the duke stopped the panic.

## scene-56 · Harold's guard

The Norman horsemen finally break into the English ranks. Harold's men fall; below, the dead are already being stripped of their mail.

## scene-57 · Harold's death

Harold is killed. But which one is he? The man clutching the arrow near his eye, or the one cut down by a horseman's sword? The "arrow in the eye" may partly be the restorers' work. Open the panel to follow the investigation.

## scene-58 · The rout

The English flee, chased by the horsemen. Here the embroidery breaks off: the end is lost, and this last caption is missing from the eighteenth-century drawings. A final scene is often imagined, William crowned at Christmas 1066. We will never see it.
